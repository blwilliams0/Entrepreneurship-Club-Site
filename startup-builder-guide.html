Start here

You do not need to solve your entire business today. The goal is to identify your current stage, the biggest uncertainty or bottleneck, and one useful action you can take next.

Seven questions to answer first

  • What are you building or trying to sell?
  • Who is it for?
  • What problem, desire, or job does it address?
  • What stage are you at?
  • What have you already done?
  • What is blocking you right now?
  • What would meaningful progress look like this week?

If you do not have an idea yet

Do not wait for a “perfect startup idea”

A strong first business can be a service, product, resale concept, local business, digital product, creative offer, or small software tool. Your first goal is an idea you can test, not an idea you can admire.

  • What are you unusually good at or willing to learn?
  • What do people already ask you for help with?
  • What group of people do you understand well?
  • What recurring problem annoys you or people around you?
  • What could you sell or test with less than $100?
  • What could you launch in one weekend?

Next: Open the club’s Idea Generation Toolkit and start with Crazy 8s. The Google Design Sprint version uses eight boxes and eight minutes to force you past your first obvious ideas.

Validate before you overbuild

  • Talk to people who actually match your target customer.
  • Ask about current behavior and pain, not just “Would you buy this?”
  • Look for evidence: existing spending, workarounds, repeated frustration, time lost, or active searching for a solution.
  • Test the riskiest assumption first.
Small tests beat big guesses

Useful tests include customer interviews, a landing page, a waitlist, a manual version of the service, a clickable prototype, a pricing test, a presale, or a simple outreach offer.

Can AI build a whole app?

For a well-scoped student MVP, AI coding tools can sometimes produce a substantial working app with a modest budget. A rough $100–$200 total tool budget may be enough for some simple projects when you do the product decisions, testing, and iteration yourself. It is not a guarantee. Complex backends, paid APIs, heavy usage, security or compliance needs, or app-store distribution can cost more. Start small and prove the core flow.

Winter Night Market quick guide

Think like a shopper

A Night Market customer should understand what the product is, who it is for, what it costs, and why it is worth buying within a few seconds.

AreaWorking guideline
PriceClub workshop heuristic: $5–$12 works well for grab-and-go or impulse items. $12–$15 can still work when value is obvious.
COGSKnow direct material cost per unit. A $10 item with roughly $3–$4 in materials leaves more room for labor, waste, and margin.
Batch sizeReality check: can you make roughly 25–40 units in one weekend without wrecking your finals schedule?
GiftabilityReady-to-gift packaging, bundles, small personalization, or clear holiday use can reduce purchase friction.
DisplayMake the product and price obvious from several feet away. Too many choices can slow the buyer down.
PaymentHave a simple payment method ready and verify event rules before the market.
Inventory riskDo not make a giant batch of an untested product. A manageable first batch is information.

The numbers above are practical club planning heuristics, not universal rules. Adjust for the product, buyer, quality, and production method.

Questions worth asking yourself

  • Why does somebody care about this?
  • Who is the first real person I would sell to?
  • How would I reach five of those people?
  • What evidence do I have that this matters?
  • What assumption could kill the idea?
  • What is the smallest useful version?
  • What should I explicitly not build yet?
  • What does one sale cost me to deliver?
  • What is my biggest bottleneck right now?
  • What can I finish in the next seven days?
